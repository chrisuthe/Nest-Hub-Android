# Fuchsia sources unlock Nest Hub retrofits

A replacement-mainboard Android build is functionally feasible for all three Nest Hubs, because Google's own open-source Fuchsia board drivers name almost every peripheral, its bus, its I2C address and its control lines, and publish the complete MIPI DSI timings and init sequences for every display variant. What is missing is entirely physical: **no public source documents any connector part, pin count, pitch or pinout, any supply or logic voltage, any backlight LED string rating, any speaker impedance, or any mainboard outline** for any of the three models. All three use 4-lane DSI portrait-native panels (600x1024 on the 7-inch models, 800x1280 on the Max), I2C touch, PDM microphones, a TCS3400 colour sensor and an LP8556 backlight driver, so one electrical architecture covers the family; the 2nd gen adds an SPI radar board with open Infineon drivers, and the Max adds a raw-Bayer Sony IMX227 camera and a three-amplifier, 24 V audio system. The stock amplifiers, backlight driver, radios and regulators sit on the mainboard and leave with it, so the new board must recreate them. On the compute side, Raspberry Pi CM5 has the newest community Android (LineageOS 23.2 / Android 16) but weak camera and no vendor audio front end, Rockchip-based Radxa modules have vendor Android 14 with device-tree panel bring-up, and NXP i.MX 8M has the best-maintained vendor Android (17) but only on evaluation kits. No completed open project has swapped the mainboard of any smart display, so this would be new ground; the 1st gen is the best-documented and simplest first target, and the first project milestone is a teardown-and-measure session, not a schematic.

## Google's own board drivers map every peripheral, but stop at the connector

The decisive source for this project is not a teardown but the Fuchsia open-source tree, which still carries board drivers for all three products under their internal names: "astro" (Nest Hub 1st gen), "nelson" (2nd gen) and "sherlock" (Hub Max). These files give SoC-side signal names, I2C addresses, bus assignments and polarity for every peripheral ([astro](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/astro/), [nelson](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/nelson/), [sherlock](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/sherlock/)), and the shared display driver publishes panel timings, lane counts and full DSI init tables for each panel and driver-IC combination ([panel-config.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/graphics/display/drivers/amlogic-display/panel-config.cc)). That removes what Raspberry Pi engineers describe as the hardest part of bringing up an unknown DSI panel, namely obtaining a trustworthy init sequence, timings and D-PHY configuration, with failure showing only as a black screen and "almost no debug information" ([Raspberry Pi forum](https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=392157)). The research notes recommend archiving these files at a pinned commit, since nothing guarantees they stay on the main branch.

Physical evidence is much thinner and uneven across models. The 1st gen has a detailed photographic teardown from EDN ([EDN, 2020](https://www.edn.com/teardown-google-home-hub-smart-speaker-and-display/)). The 2nd gen has a hobbyist teardown with chip markings and logic-analyser captures of the radar ([Hackaday.io project](https://hackaday.io/project/190147-google-nest-hub-2nd-gen-teardown)) and a summary of a TechInsights teardown ([Electronics360, 2021](https://electronics360.globalspec.com/article/17053/teardown-google-nest-hub-2nd-gen)). The Hub Max has no component-level teardown at all; its only public internal imagery is a 10-page FCC internal-photo exhibit ([fccid.io A4RH2A](https://fccid.io/A4RH2A/Internal-Photos/Internal-Photos-rev-4255949)). FCC internal photos also exist for the 1st gen ([fccid.io A4RH1A](https://fccid.io/A4RH1A)) and 2nd gen ([fccid.io A4RGUIK2](https://fccid.io/A4RGUIK2)), but the schematics and block diagrams are under long-term confidentiality, and none of the photo exhibits was visually inspected in this research. Reviewing those three PDFs is the cheapest remaining desk-research step.

Panel variants, strap logic and timings come from the Fuchsia panel-type list and board post-init code ([display-panel.h](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/graphics/display/lib/device-protocol-display/include/lib/device-protocol/display-panel.h), [astro post-init](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/astro/post-init/post-init.cc), [nelson post-init](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/nelson/post-init/post-init.cc), [sherlock post-init](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/sherlock/post-init/post-init.cc)). I2C addresses come from the per-board I2C maps ([astro.h](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/astro/astro.h), [nelson.h](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/nelson/nelson.h), [sherlock-i2c.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/sherlock/sherlock-i2c.cc)), and 2nd-gen part markings from the Hackaday.io component list ([Hackaday.io](https://hackaday.io/project/190147/components)). Speaker and power ratings come from Google's spec pages and reviews ([Google support](https://support.google.com/googlehome/answer/7072284?hl=en), [Google Store](https://store.google.com/gb/product/google_nest_hub_max_tech_specs?hl=en-GB), [GadgetGuy, 2019](https://www.gadgetguy.com.au/google-nest-hub-max-adding-vision-to-voice-commands/)).

Three family-wide facts shape the design. First, **every panel is portrait-native and mounted landscape**, so the new system must rotate the framebuffer 90 degrees; the marketing resolutions of 1024x600 and 1280x800 describe use, not scan order. Second, **every unit has two or more possible panel and driver-IC combinations**, selected on the stock board by strap pins on the display module, so a replacement board should read those straps or the builder must identify the variant in hand. Third, the DSI link is undemanding: by the researchers' arithmetic from the published timings, the 7-inch panels need roughly 265 to 300 Mbps per lane and the 10-inch about 424 Mbps per lane, within the JD936x limit of 500 Mbps per lane that the Fuchsia comments quote ([panel-config.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/graphics/display/drivers/amlogic-display/panel-config.cc)). Display bandwidth therefore does not discriminate between compute modules.

## The amplifier, backlight driver and radios leave with the mainboard

The value of the retrofit rests on which parts live off the mainboard. Where that is confirmed, it is favourable; where it is not, it is the first thing to check on a real unit.

### Nest Hub 1st gen: four cables and a passive power board

EDN's teardown shows the cleanest partition of the three. Exactly four interconnects leave the mainboard: **one flex to the glued display assembly, one flex to a top daughterboard carrying both MEMS microphones, the ambient light sensor and the mute switch, one flex to a base board holding the barrel jack and a hidden micro-USB port, and a two-wire speaker cable**. The base board "contains no active circuitry, only signal routing traces", which means 14 V and USB arrive at the mainboard over a flex and all regulation is on the board being discarded ([EDN](https://www.edn.com/teardown-google-home-hub-smart-speaker-and-display/)).

A replacement board for this model must therefore provide a 4-lane DSI output with an LCD reset line and one panel-ID input; an I2C bus with interrupt and reset for touch, answering at 0x38 on BOE units or 0x5D on Innolux units ([astro touch.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/astro/post-init/touch.cc)); an I2C bus plus interrupt for the TCS3400 at 0x39 ([astro-light.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/astro/astro-light.cc)); one PDM clock output and one PDM data input carrying two channels ([astro-audio.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/astro/astro-audio.cc)); a mono class-D amplifier fed by I2S to replace the TAS2770-family part; GPIO inputs for volume up, volume down and the mute switch, which the stock board reads as plain active-low or active-high levels with pull-ups ([astro-buttons.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/astro/astro-buttons.cc)); an optional PWM for the amber LED; and conversion from 14 V to all logic rails. The stock backlight setup is a known quantity: an LP8556 at 0x2C with seven published register writes ([astro backlight.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/astro/post-init/backlight.cc)). Because that IC is configured on a mainboard I2C bus, the researchers infer it sits on the mainboard and only LED strings cross the display flex, which would mean the new board needs its own LED boost driver; this is unconfirmed. The touch controller follows the panel vendor and needs no firmware download on this model, which strongly suggests it lives on the display module and survives the swap, also unconfirmed visually. The micro-USB port on the passive base board offers a ready-made service or debug USB route for the new board once the flex is traced.

### Nest Hub 2nd gen: the same core plus radar and a third microphone

The 2nd gen repeats the 1st-gen architecture with different parts. Touch moves to a **Goodix GT6853**, which loads a panel-specific configuration chosen by driver-IC family; a Fuchsia comment notes "there's a chance we can proceed without a config", so the controller's factory-flashed configuration likely suffices, but this needs a test ([gt6853-nelson.cc snapshot](https://go-mod-viewer.appspot.com/go.fuchsia.dev/fuchsia@v0.0.0-20211206140801-bbc9ce36f629/src/ui/input/drivers/gt6853/gt6853-nelson.cc)). The researchers flag that this chip belongs to a newer Goodix protocol family than the common GT9xx parts and that Linux driver coverage is an unverified lead, making touch the main software risk on this model. The amplifier is a **TAS5805M wired as a bridged mono output**, with a separate inrush or boost enable line and two INA231 power monitors on the stock board ([nelson-audio.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/nelson/nelson-audio.cc)); the stock EQ and limiter tuning is compiled from a non-public header, so speaker protection must be re-derived conservatively.

Three microphones share one PDM clock, two on the first data line and the third on a second ([nelson-audio.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/nelson/nelson-audio.cc)). TechInsights counts only two microphones on the separate sensor board alongside the Sensirion temperature and humidity sensor ([Electronics360](https://electronics360.globalspec.com/article/17053/teardown-google-nest-hub-2nd-gen)), which implies **the third microphone sits elsewhere, possibly on the mainboard, and would then be lost**. The locations of the TCS3400, the volume switches, the amber LED and the barrel jack are likewise undocumented for this model.

The Soli radar is the best-documented reusable part in the whole family. It is an **Infineon BGT60TR13C on its own small board with an 80 MHz oscillator and 1.8 V LDO**, connected by four-wire SPI plus interrupt and reset, clocked by the stock board at 30.3 MHz ([nelson-spi.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/nelson/nelson-spi.cc), [Hackaday.io components](https://hackaday.io/project/190147/components)). The Hackaday.io author captured the stock configuration, about 38 register writes, and the readout loop of roughly 30 bursts per second, and published the logic-analyser files; the radar board's pinout exists publicly only as two photographs in that project ([Hackaday.io log](https://hackaday.io/project/190147-google-nest-hub-2nd-gen-teardown), [pinout log](https://hackaday.io/project/190147/log/216912-bgt60tr13c-radar-pcb-pinout)). Infineon publishes a platform-independent driver library that needs only SPI, chip select, interrupt and reset ([Infineon sensor-xensiv-bgt60trxx](https://github.com/Infineon/sensor-xensiv-bgt60trxx)) and an open presence-detection library ([Infineon radar presence example](https://github.com/Infineon/mtb-example-psoc6-radar-presence)). The realistic outcome is presence, motion and range sensing on the new board; Google's gesture and sleep-tracking algorithms are proprietary and do not transfer. The product's FCC grant covers 58 to 63.5 GHz operation under a specific waiver ([fccid.io A4RGUIK2](https://fccid.io/A4RGUIK2)), so running the radar with a non-stock configuration is a regulatory question worth noting before the brainstorm.

Power for the 2nd gen is the least certain of the three. Google states only a 15 W adapter and DC jack ([Google support](https://support.google.com/googlehome/answer/7072284?hl=en)); 14 V at 1.1 A is the likely figure by analogy with the 1st gen and marketplace listings, but no primary source confirms it.

### Nest Hub Max: camera, three amplifiers and 24 V raise the bar

The Max adds two subsystems that materially increase effort. The camera is a **Sony IMX227 on 2-lane CSI-2 at 1000 Mbps per lane, RAW10, with a 24 MHz master clock**, a reset line and two regulator-enable lines, which shows the sensor's analog and digital supplies are generated on the mainboard and must be rebuilt ([sherlock-camera.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/sherlock/sherlock-camera.cc), [imx227_modes.h](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/camera/drivers/sensors/imx227/imx227_modes.h), [imx227.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/camera/drivers/sensors/imx227/imx227.cc)). A raw Bayer sensor needs an image signal processor and, with a 127-degree lens, dewarping, which the stock board does in Amlogic hardware. The Fuchsia driver's register tables are the only public init sequences found; no mainline Linux driver for this sensor was identified, and no datasheet was located. The camera is therefore the highest-risk peripheral in the family, and treating it as a later phase or leaving it dark is a legitimate scoping choice.

Audio is a three-way active system. Each driver has its own TAS5720 amplifier on a shared I2C bus, fed by two I2S data lines, with the woofer receiving a mono mix and the tweeters running 6.4 dB lower, crossed over digitally at 1.2 kHz ([sherlock-audio.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/sherlock/sherlock-audio.cc)). The researchers infer there is no passive crossover in the enclosure, so a replacement must provide three amplifier channels and a crossover in DSP or software, and full-range signal into the tweeters would damage them. The 24 V, 30 W supply exists mainly for these amplifiers. The display side adds an unidentified "lcd_bias" I2C device at 0x3E next to the LP8556 ([sherlock-i2c.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/sherlock/sherlock-i2c.cc)), probably a dual-rail bias supply, and the touch controller is flagged as needing firmware from the host, although a unit that has run stock software likely retains it.

The Max's physical partition is unknown. No source states where the mainboard sits, whether the microphones, light sensor, LEDs, buttons, privacy switch or DC jack are on it, or whether the privacy switch cuts camera and microphone power in hardware; the software sees a single GPIO ([sherlock-buttons.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/sherlock/sherlock-buttons.cc)). One helpful detail is that the stock Wi-Fi module uses MHF4 antenna connectors, implying reusable coax-fed antennas ([WikiDevi](https://wikidevi.wi-cat.ru/Google_Nest_Hub_Max_(H2A))). The researchers' estimate of the minimum host interface is a 4-lane DSI, a 2-lane CSI with clock, two or three I2C buses, two I2S data outputs, one PDM pair, about twelve GPIOs and optionally three ADC inputs for thermistors.

## Compute choice turns on Android freshness versus audio and camera plumbing

Every candidate module has enough DSI capacity for these panels, so the choice rests on Android maintenance, panel bring-up method, audio input and, for the Max, camera support.

**Raspberry Pi CM5** has the most current community Android. KonstaKANG's LineageOS 23.2 (Android 16 QPR2) explicitly supports the CM5, with a latest build dated 20 May 2026; it offers working GPU and Vulkan, I2S DAC output, I2C sensors including an ambient-light part, and USB microphones, but SELinux is permissive, storage encryption is unsupported, CSI cameras have "various issues", only two preset DSI panels are selectable, and its licence forbids commercial use ([KonstaKANG](https://konstakang.com/devices/rpi5/LineageOS23/)). The module is 55 x 40 mm with two 4-lane MIPI ports usable as CSI or DSI ([CM5 datasheet](https://pip.raspberrypi.com/documents/RP-008180-DS-cm5-datasheet.pdf)). A custom panel on Pi needs a kernel panel driver or simple-panel entry plus an overlay ([Raspberry Pi forum](https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=392157), [raspberrypi/firmware #1819](https://github.com/raspberrypi/firmware/issues/1819)), so the Android image must be rebuilt from source. Cost has moved against the Pi: memory-driven price rises in February, April and November 2026 took the 2 GB Lite CM5 from $55 to $67.50 and pushed 16 GB variants to about $375 ([price tracker](https://raspberry.tips/en/raspberrypi-infos/raspberry-pi-prices-lifecycle), [Raspberry Pi news](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/price-rises-for-some-16gb-raspberry-pi-products/)). The Pi ecosystem has the strongest carrier-design prior art, including Raspberry Pi's open CM4IO KiCad files and antmicro's Apache-licensed baseboard, which is electrically compatible with the CM5 apart from the pins the CM5 reassigned from the 2-lane MIPI port to USB 3 ([DigiKey/Shawn Hymel](https://www.digikey.com/en/maker/projects/creating-a-raspberry-pi-compute-module-4-cm4-carrier-board-in-kicad/7812da347e5e409aa28d59ea2aaea490), [antmicro cm4-baseboard](https://github.com/antmicro/cm4-baseboard)).

**Radxa CM4 (RK3576) and CM5 (RK3588S2)** trade Android freshness for vendor integration. Radxa's releases are one-off Android 14 drops from 2025, including a CM4 image built for a Radxa DSI LCD, with no visible patch cadence ([radxa/manifests releases](https://github.com/radxa/manifests/releases)). The Rockchip BSP describes a DSI panel, including reset GPIO and lane rate, in device tree rather than C code ([youyeetoo wiki](https://wiki.youyeetoo.com/en/tinker/page/DebianSystem/mipi-dsi)), which suits transcribing the Fuchsia init tables. The Radxa CM4 launched at $70 and up ([Liliputing](https://liliputing.com/radxa-cm4-with-an-rk3576-processor-could-be-a-cheaper-alternative-to-the-radxa-cm5-with-rk3588/)), and the Radxa CM5 takes a 3.6 to 5.2 V input ([Radxa CM5 docs](https://docs.radxa.com/en/som/cm/cm5)). A hobbyist handheld built on the Radxa CM5 with a custom DSI panel board shows the route is achievable ([Retro-Lite-CM5](https://github.com/StonedEdge/Retro-Lite-CM5)). The researchers' unverified background is that Rockchip and i.MX parts have native multi-channel PDM inputs while the Pi does not expose one under Android; if confirmed, that favours them for reusing the stock microphones directly.

**NXP i.MX 8M Mini and Plus** have the best-maintained vendor Android, with Android 17 released in November 2026 on a 6.18 kernel and public sources, but NXP supports only its own evaluation kits and each SoM vendor ports with its own delay ([NXP Android for i.MX](https://www.nxp.com/design/design-center/software/embedded-software/i-mx-software/android-os-for-i-mx-applications-processors:IMXANDROID)). No hobby-priced SoM was researched. **Qualcomm QCS6490** boards offer Android 13 to 16 but as single-board computers or commercial SoMs rather than hobby modules ([Radxa Dragon Q6A](https://docs.radxa.com/en/dragon/q6a), [Thundercomm](https://www.thundercomm.com/product/rubik-pi/)).

Audio capture deserves its own decision independent of the SoC. An **XMOS XVF3800 front end** takes four PDM microphones and performs echo cancellation, beamforming and noise suppression on-chip, presenting clean audio over USB or I2S ([Seeed](https://www.seeed.cc/product/respeaker-mic-array-v2-0), [creatingsmarthome.com, 2026](https://www.creatingsmarthome.com/index.php/2026/04/12/home-assistant-diy-voice-assistant-with-seeed-studio-respeaker-xvf3800/)); over USB it lands on a path KonstaKANG's Android already supports. Whether XMOS firmware suits the stock two- or three-microphone geometry was not researched. On the output side, the TAS5805M has a working out-of-tree Linux driver ([Sonocotta driver](https://github.com/sonocotta/tas5805m-for-raspbian-paspberry-pi-zero)), and the MAX98357A works as a configuration-free I2S amplifier with known start/stop pops ([Adafruit](https://learn.adafruit.com/adafruit-max98357-i2s-class-d-mono-amp/raspberry-pi-usage)).

Prior art sets expectations. Drop-in replacement boards exist for Google's smart speakers, such as the ESP32-based MiciMike board for the Nest Mini that reuses the enclosure, speaker, supply and flex cable ([nest-mini-drop-in-pcb](https://github.com/iMike78/nest-mini-drop-in-pcb)), but for smart displays the research found only discussion threads and a teardown author's stated intention to try a Pi compute module in an Echo Show 5 ([Brian Dorey](https://www.briandorey.com/post/echo-show-5-2nd-gen-smart-display-teardown)). The Android application layer for a Home Assistant style display, by contrast, is mature and needs only working touch, microphone and speaker ([Ava Pro](https://github.com/knoop7/Ava-Pro), [DashVoice](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/i-built-a-fully-kiosk-alternative-with-on-device-voice-control-meet-dashvoice/980581)). Board fabrication is not the obstacle either: CM-class carriers are routinely built on four layers, and JLCPCB offers controlled impedance on multilayer boards at no extra charge ([JLCPCB](https://jlcpcb.com/impedance)).

## A caliper, multimeter and microscope session gates the schematic

Nothing below was found in any public text source, for any model, and each item blocks a design decision. The list is the measurement plan for the first physical unit.

Connectors come first: the type, pitch, pin count, contact side and pinout of the display flex, any separate touch tail, the microphone and sensor daughterboard flex, the radar flex on the 2nd gen, the camera connector on the Max, the base power flex on the 1st gen, the speaker connectors and the antenna connectors. For the radar board alone, two published photographs give a starting point ([Hackaday.io pinout log](https://hackaday.io/project/190147/log/216912-bgt60tr13c-radar-pcb-pinout)). Next come voltages: the logic level of each bus, which on these Amlogic designs can be 1.8 V or 3.3 V per bank, the panel supply rails and any bias rails, the touch supply, the daughterboard supply, the radar board's input rail and the camera rails. The Fuchsia display code references a shared panel power-on sequence table that the researchers did not transcribe; reading it is a desk task that should precede measurement ([panel-config.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/graphics/display/drivers/amlogic-display/panel-config.cc)).

The backlight needs the LED string count, forward voltage and current. The published LP8556 register values encode the current and string configuration and can be decoded against TI's datasheet, a decode not yet done, and the boost output can be measured on a working unit before disassembly ([astro backlight.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/astro/post-init/backlight.cc), [sherlock backlight.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/sherlock/post-init/backlight.cc)). Speakers need DC resistance and any markings, since impedance and safe power are undocumented for all three; the only figures are Google's driver sizes and the Max's nominal wattages. Power input needs the adapter label, barrel dimensions and polarity; the widely quoted 3.5 x 1.35 mm plug size rests only on marketplace listings.

Component location must be settled by inspection: whether the LP8556 and, on the Max, the bias IC sit on the mainboard or the display flex; whether the touch controller is on the flex; where the 2nd gen's third microphone, light sensor, buttons and LED sit; where every Max peripheral sits; and whether the mute and privacy switches cut power or clock in hardware or merely signal the SoC. Finally the mechanical envelope must be captured: mainboard outline, mounting holes, connector positions, height limits, heat-spreader contact areas, the positions of any board-mounted switches that poke through the enclosure, and the antenna arrangement. Disassembly itself carries risk on the 1st gen, where the display is glued and EDN's author cracked the glass prying it off ([EDN](https://www.edn.com/teardown-google-home-hub-smart-speaker-and-display/)). Capturing I2C traffic and rail voltages from a unit while it still runs stock firmware is passive observation and yields the touch, backlight and amplifier behaviour without touching that firmware.

## Seven decisions the brainstorm must settle

The first decision is **which model to target first, and whether to pursue one board or three**. The 1st gen has the best teardown, the simplest audio, no radar or camera, commonly supported touch controller families and a known 14 V supply, making it the natural pathfinder. The 2nd gen shares its panel family and bus layout and adds only the well-documented radar, the GT6853 touch risk and the third microphone. The Max differs in voltage, panel size, amplifier count and camera, and is undocumented mechanically, so it warrants its own board. A shared schematic core across the two 7-inch models is plausible; a shared outline is unknowable until both boards are measured.

The second is **the compute module**, which is really a choice between the newest Android with a community image and a C-driver panel port (CM5), an older vendor Android with device-tree panel description and vendor media and audio blocks (Radxa RK3576 or RK3588S), and a current vendor Android with the heaviest integration burden (i.MX 8M). How much Play Store access, security patching and long-term image maintenance matter to the user decides this more than performance does. The third is **the microphone path**: stock PDM microphones straight into the SoC with software echo cancellation, or through an XMOS-class voice processor, or abandoned in favour of an added array. The fourth is **the amplifier strategy**: like-for-like parts that match the stock topology (TAS2770, TAS5805M bridged, three TAS5720) or a simpler common part, and how to protect small drivers without Google's tuning; on the Max this includes where the 1.2 kHz crossover runs.

The fifth is **whether to reuse the LP8556 with Google's published register values**, which is the low-risk path if the LED strings are exposed to the mainboard, or to design a different driver once the strings are characterised. The sixth is **radio and antenna handling**: on-module Wi-Fi with its own antenna, or a module wired to the enclosure's stock antennas, and whether to restore Thread, which the 2nd gen and Max implement with Nordic radios on the stock mainboard ([nelson-spi.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/nelson/nelson-spi.cc), [sherlock-spi.cc](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/fuchsia/+/refs/heads/main/src/devices/board/drivers/sherlock/sherlock-spi.cc)). The seventh is **scope of optional peripherals and thermal headroom**: whether the radar ships in version one as a presence sensor, whether the Max camera is attempted at all, and whether a hotter module can live in an enclosure the researchers judge was designed, with a heat sink and thermal tape on the 1st gen, for an SoC dissipating a few watts within a 15 W total budget. That thermal judgement is engineering opinion, not a sourced figure, and needs validation with the chosen module.

A process decision sits above all seven: the researchers recommend a staged approach, proving panel, touch and audio on an off-the-shelf module IO board through small flex adapter boards before committing to a full form-fit carrier. Given that panel bring-up failures present as a silent black screen, that staging converts the largest single risk into a cheap experiment.

## Conclusion

The research inverts the usual shape of a salvage project. Normally the display init sequence is the unknowable secret and the mechanics are merely tedious; here Google has published the secrets and the tedium is all that remains. That makes the project's critical path a bench session rather than a reverse-engineering campaign, and it makes a sacrificial unit of each target model, ideally one still booting stock firmware for passive rail and bus measurements, the most valuable purchase before any design work.

It also reframes what "reuse" delivers. The screen, touch, microphones, speakers, light sensor, radar board and enclosure carry over; the things that made the product sound and behave like a Nest Hub, namely the amplifier tuning, far-field voice processing, camera pipeline and radar algorithms, do not, and each must be consciously rebuilt, substituted or dropped. A brainstorm that treats the 1st gen as a pathfinder for a shared 7-inch electrical core, defers the Max's camera, and decides early how much voice quality matters will converge fastest. Several leads were left unverified and are worth a short follow-up before committing: Linux driver coverage for the GT6853, FT5726, TCS3400 and IMX227, Google's GPL kernel source drops for these products, the three FCC internal-photo exhibits, and whether a newer community Android build exists than the May 2026 one found.
